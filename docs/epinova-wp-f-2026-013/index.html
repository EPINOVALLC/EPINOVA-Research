The Limits of Strategic Reach

Commitment Overhang, Power Conversion, and the Erosion of Strategic Optionality

  • Wu, Shaoyuan
    Global AI Governance and Policy Research Center, EPINOVA LLC
    https://orcid.org/0009-0008-0660-8232

Description

This working paper develops a diagnostic framework for analyzing the limits of strategic reach through commitment overhang, strategic free capacity, and strategic optionality. It argues that great-power sustainability cannot be assessed through fiscal affordability or aggregate national power alone because strategic commitments draw on partially nonfungible resources and can impose different temporal burdens. The paper distinguishes high-burn commitments that consume current capability from lock-in commitments that encumber future capacity, and integrates protection recursion, regeneration bottlenecks, portfolio interaction, competitive expansion feedback, control-induced diversification, and justificatory portability. The 2026 U.S.–Israel–Iran conflict illustrates burn and restoration liabilities; Greenland illustrates prospective lock-in; and Canada–EU cooperation illustrates diversification intent. The paper concludes that strategic reach can continue to expand while discretionary capacity for future contingencies contracts, and that negative net conversion, declining free capacity, and loss of strategic optionality must be treated as distinct analytical outcomes.

Abstract

Great-power overstretch is often framed as a mismatch between external commitments and economic resources. This paper develops a diagnostic framework centered on three linked concepts: commitment overhang, strategic free capacity, and strategic optionality. It integrates partially nonfungible resource bottlenecks, burn and lock-in portfolio effects, and external feedback. Additional strategic reach generates usable power only when cumulative converted value exceeds lifecycle and systemic burden and when sustainment and regeneration preserve minimum reserves. The 2026 U.S.–Israel–Iran conflict illustrates high-burn depletion; Greenland illustrates potential lock-in while distinguishing existing Pituffik obligations and pre-existing modernization from uncertain incremental commitments; and Canada–EU cooperation illustrates diversification intent without presuming measurable U.S. leverage loss. The paper uses the strategic poison pill only as a diagnostic metaphor for negative cumulative net conversion, distinct from optionality loss. The paper neither estimates a universal threshold nor claims that any case has crossed one. Its contribution is to integrate commitment portfolios, capacity regeneration, and external adaptation into a framework for explaining how strategic reach can expand while discretionary capacity for future contingencies contracts.
