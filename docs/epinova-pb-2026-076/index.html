If Strikes Resume

Military Readiness, Regeneration, and Strategic Optionality in the U.S.–Israel–Iran Conflict

  • Wu, Shaoyuan
    Global AI Governance and Policy Research Center, EPINOVA LLC
    https://orcid.org/0009-0008-0660-8232

Description

This policy brief examines the readiness, force regeneration, and strategic optionality of the United States, Israel, and Iran amid preparations for possible renewed strikes. It separates military preparation from political authorization and short-term tactical effects from sustainable strategic outcomes. Drawing on public evidence and conditional production–launch sensitivity tests, it compares offensive capabilities, missile-defense replenishment, Iran’s uncertain missile and drone production recovery, and cross-theater commitments. The brief considers three pathways—continued armed bargaining, limited strikes and retaliation, and sustained reciprocal attrition—alongside regional and maritime spillover, Gulf defenses, diplomacy, and energy-route alternatives. It argues that a tactically successful operation need not improve net strategic conversion or preserve future options; those judgments require evidence of durable effects, replenishment burdens, and genuinely available capacity.

Abstract

This policy brief examines the military readiness, force regeneration, and strategic optionality of the United States, Israel, and Iran amid preparations for possible renewed conflict in November 2026. It distinguishes military preparations from political authorization and immediate strike capabilities from sustainable strategic outcomes. Drawing on publicly available reporting, defense assessments, and conditional sensitivity analysis, the study evaluates U.S. and Israeli offensive and defensive constraints, Iran’s uncertain missile and drone production recovery, and the implications of interceptor replenishment and regional commitments. It compares three prospective pathways: continued armed bargaining, limited renewed strikes and retaliation, and sustained reciprocal attrition, while considering potential regional and maritime spillover. The analysis further examines Gulf defense arrangements, diplomatic mediation, and alternative energy routes as factors shaping strategic options and costs. It argues that tactical success does not necessarily produce positive net strategic conversion or preserve future strategic optionality. Sustainable outcomes depend on verified operational effects, regeneration capacity, replenishment burdens, institutional commitments, and the ability to retain feasible responses to subsequent contingencies. The scenarios are conditional and do not constitute predictions of renewed strikes or their outcomes.
