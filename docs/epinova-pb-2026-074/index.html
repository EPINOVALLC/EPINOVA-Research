South Korea’s AI Power Under Uneven Interdependence

Semiconductor Depth, National Conversion, and the KIC Model-Selection Case

  • Wu, Shaoyuan
    Global AI Governance and Policy Research Center, EPINOVA LLC
    https://orcid.org/0009-0008-0660-8232

Description

This policy brief examines South Korea’s AI power under uneven technological interdependence. It argues that Korea combines globally consequential semiconductor and high-bandwidth-memory capabilities with growing domestic models, public compute programs, cloud and telecommunications capacity, and industrial and administrative AI applications, but these strengths do not constitute a nationally controlled end-to-end AI stack. The brief distinguishes AI National Power, AI Boundary Power, Boundary Autonomy, and Systemic Influence, and evaluates Korea’s capacity conversion across memory, compute, domestic models, industrial AI, and public-sector deployment. It uses the Korea Investment Corporation as a diagnostic case of mixed-origin institutional AI, where Chinese-developed Qwen3 and BGE-M3 reportedly operate alongside U.S.-developed models for differentiated internal functions. The case does not establish foreign control, strategic realignment, or durable dependence; instead, it highlights the importance of deployment architecture, continuing control rights, switching costs, security review, and function-preserving substitution. The brief concludes that South Korea’s future AI power will depend on converting specialized technological depth into sustainable national functions, exercising effective control at consequential interfaces, and preserving credible alternatives under disruption.

Abstract

South Korea combines globally consequential AI-memory manufacturing with domestic models, public-private compute allocation, advanced cloud and telecommunications organizations, and manufacturing applications. These strengths do not comprise a nationally controlled end-to-end AI stack. Advanced accelerators, semiconductor equipment, packaging relationships, model software, and overseas production sites create external interfaces over which Korean actors exercise only partial authority. The analytical question is how Korean institutions convert heterogeneous resources into functions they can access and sustain, while retaining credible alternatives where consequential interfaces are controlled elsewhere. KIC provides a diagnostic institutional case. Its reported use of Chinese-developed language and retrieval models is compatible with substantial domestic model-development capacity and close U.S.-linked semiconductor and security relationships. Yet reported use volumes, the timing of its GPU expansion contract, and unknown system architecture prevent strong claims about which models are operationally indispensable, whether foreign actors possess continuing technical control, or whether cross-border adoption has become systemic influence. The practical implication is not model-origin neutrality at any cost: it is function-based procurement subject to verifiable security, legal, and continuity controls.
