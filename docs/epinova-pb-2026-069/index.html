From AI-Enabled Weapons to AI-Orchestrated Warfare

The Emerging Global Military AI Stack in 2026

  • Wu, Shaoyuan
    Global AI Governance and Policy Research Center, EPINOVA LLC
    https://orcid.org/0009-0008-0660-8232

Description

This policy brief examines the transition from AI-enabled weapons to AI-orchestrated warfare. It argues that the main unit of military AI competition is shifting from individual models or weapons to a broader military AI stack that integrates compute, trusted data, models, command platforms, sensors, weapons, autonomous systems, and allied networks. The brief identifies seven stack layers—energy and compute, data and models, integration and digital environments, command decision support, firepower orchestration, edge autonomy, and alliance governance—and compares major pathways across the United States, NATO, China, U.S. allies, Ukraine, Russia, and Israel. It concludes that the most consequential effect of military AI is decision compression: AI can shape what commanders see, how threats are ranked, which options appear feasible, and how quickly decisions must be made, even when humans retain formal authorization.

Abstract

Military artificial intelligence is entering a new phase. Public debate has often focused on AI-enabled weapons such as autonomous drones, machine-vision targeting, loitering munitions, and robotic platforms. Those systems remain important, but the principal direction of change is toward AI-orchestrated warfare: the integration of compute, military data, models, command platforms, sensors, weapons, autonomous systems, and allied networks into a continuously operating warfighting architecture. This brief introduces a seven-layer military AI stack covering energy and compute, data and models, integration and digital environments, command decision support, firepower orchestration, edge autonomy, and alliance governance. It compares pathways visible in the United States, NATO, China, Japan, South Korea, the United Kingdom, Germany, Ukraine, Russia, and Israel. The central strategic effect of the stack is decision compression. AI can compress information, decision time, organizational processes, choices, and operational sequences even when humans retain formal command authority. Military AI governance should therefore move beyond a narrow focus on fully autonomous weapons and cover decision-support platforms, target-generation systems, model updates, data provenance, supplier dependence, review time, human override, and degraded operations.
