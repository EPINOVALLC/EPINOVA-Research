AI in China's Higher Education: From National Strategy to Educational Value

Faculty Development, Disciplinary Implementation, and the Evidence Needed to Assess Learning

  • Wu, Shaoyuan
    Global AI Governance and Policy Research Center, EPINOVA LLC
    https://orcid.org/0009-0008-0660-8232

Description

This policy brief examines how China's national AI strategy is being translated into higher education policy, faculty development, and discipline-specific teaching. Drawing on official policy documents, university training notices, international guidance, and sixteen selected faculty training session summaries, it identifies the gap between AI adoption and demonstrated educational value. The analysis compares teaching applications across the humanities, social sciences, economics, archaeology, laboratories, and physical education, emphasizing disciplinary validity, teacher oversight, student autonomy, and safety. It proposes distinct evidence layers for access, implementation, assisted performance, and durable independent capability, alongside safeguards for privacy and equity. The brief argues that training certificates, usage metrics, efficiency gains, and polished AI outputs cannot substitute for independently verified learning, retention, transfer, and responsible educational decisions.

Abstract

This policy brief examines how China's national artificial intelligence strategy is being translated into higher education policy, faculty development, and disciplinary teaching practices. Drawing on official policy documents, university training notices, international guidance, and sixteen selected faculty training session summaries, it analyzes the relationship between institutional implementation and educational value. The cases span humanities, social sciences, economics, archaeology, experimental teaching, and physical education, highlighting differences in disciplinary validity, human oversight, and safety requirements. The brief distinguishes AI access, implementation fidelity, assisted task performance, and durable independent learning as separate levels of evidence. It argues that training participation, adoption rates, efficiency gains, and polished AI outputs do not independently demonstrate educational effectiveness. Policy recommendations address evidence quality, faculty competence, institutional accountability, technical governance, equitable access, and risk proportionate evaluation. The analysis concludes that China's next challenge is to convert expanding AI integration into verifiable improvements in learning, independent reasoning, knowledge transfer, and educational opportunity, while preserving innovation and appropriate safeguards.
