ASEAN in the Emerging AI Power Order

Distributed National Nodes, Overlapping Technological Systems, and Strategic Autonomy

  • Wu, Shaoyuan
    Global AI Governance and Policy Research Center, EPINOVA LLC
    https://orcid.org/0009-0008-0660-8232

Description

This policy brief examines ASEAN’s place in the emerging AI power order as a distributed regional coordination and overlap environment rather than a unified AI power. It distinguishes AI National Power at the member-state level, AI Boundary Power at specific controller–target–interface relations, Boundary Autonomy as the ability to preserve functions through credible alternatives, and Systemic Influence as a separate outcome requiring persistent, sufficiently broad, and credibly attributable effects. The brief compares the differentiated functional roles of ASEAN’s eleven members, analyzes four regional mechanisms—aggregation, interoperability, option-set expansion, and mediation—and examines overlap among U.S., Chinese, domestic, Japanese, Korean, European, Indian, and Gulf-linked technological systems. A diagnostic Malaysia–Singapore advanced-compute case illustrates how locally held legal and regulatory interfaces can become strategically consequential without creating a unified ASEAN enforcement regime. The brief concludes that ASEAN’s strategic relevance lies in interoperable pluralism: preserving multiple workable connections, strengthening nationally controlled interfaces, and converting coordination and investment into operational interoperability, credible alternatives, and durable local capability.

Abstract

Southeast Asia is often portrayed as a contested space between U.S. and Chinese artificial-intelligence ecosystems. This brief argues that ASEAN is best understood as a distributed regional coordination and overlap environment. AI National Power remains attributable to member-state systems and their usable, mobilizable functions. AI Boundary Power is relational; Boundary Autonomy describes a target's credible ability to switch, substitute, bypass, or internalize at an interface; and Systemic Influence requires a separate showing of persistent, sufficiently broad, credibly attributable effects. Four mechanisms warrant attention: aggregation of complementary nodes, interoperability among systems, expansion of credible national options, and mediation across external technology and governance agendas. The immediate policy opportunity is interoperable pluralism: preserving multiple workable connections, securing nationally controlled interfaces, and converting investments into capabilities that can operate across organizational and technological boundaries.
